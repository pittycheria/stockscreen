How the score is calculated — plain-language explainer

Every stock gets a score from 0 to 100. It is a weighted blend of five "pillars," each of which also runs 0–100. Think of each pillar as answering one question:

PillarWeightThe question it answers
Value35% Is this stock cheap compared to companies in its own sector? We look at three "yields" — earnings expected next year, cash operating profits (EBITDA), and free cash flow, each divided by what you pay. Higher yield = more business per dollar. A bank is only ever compared with banks, a software company with software companies — otherwise the list would just fill up with industries that always look cheap.
Momentum20% Is the market warming up to the stock or giving up on it? We measure the share-price move over the past 12 months, skipping the most recent month (a standard convention). This matters in a value screen because the classic mistake is buying something cheap that keeps getting cheaper — the "falling knife."
Quality20% Is this a good business or a cheap-for-a-reason business? Profitability (return on assets, profit margins), balance-sheet strength (debt load, ability to pay near-term bills), all measured against sector peers on a sliding scale — no arbitrary pass/fail lines.
Reverse DCF15% How much growth is already baked into the price? Instead of guessing what a company is "worth," we solve the opposite problem: what yearly free-cash-flow growth over the next five years would justify today's price? If the answer is "shrink 5% a year," the bar is on the floor — that scores well. If the answer is "grow 12% a year," a lot has to go right — that scores poorly. (Not applicable to banks and REITs, whose cash flows don't work this way.)
Analyst upside10% What do Wall Street analysts think it's worth? The gap between the average analyst price target and today's price. This gets the smallest weight on purpose: targets are known to be optimistic on average and slow to change.

Reading a pillar score. Pillar scores are percentile-like: 50 means "typical for its comparison group," 84 means roughly one standard deviation better than typical, 16 one worse. Extreme outliers are capped so a single weird data point can't dominate.

Worked example. Say a stock scores Value 80, Momentum 55, Quality 70, Reverse DCF 90, Upside 40. The blend is 80×35% + 55×20% + 70×20% + 90×15% + 40×10% = 72.5. If it also carries one risk flag (say, leverage), 4 points come off: final score 68.5. When a pillar can't be computed (a bank has no reverse DCF), the remaining pillars are re-weighted proportionally rather than the stock being punished for it.

Risk flags are simple warnings — heavy debt, a dividend eating nearly all earnings, negative earnings, bottom-decile momentum, very few analysts covering, and so on. Each one subtracts 4 points and is spelled out in the Flags column, so you can see why a score was docked, not just that it was.

What the score is not. It is not a price target, a prediction, or investment advice. It is a ranking device: a high score means "cheap versus its sector, decent business, market not fleeing, low expectations bar" — which is a good place to start research, not to end it. Stocks can be cheap for excellent reasons the data can't see (lawsuits, disruption, management). Implied 5y gr. — the reverse-DCF output: the annual FCF growth over five years needed to justify today's price. Negative means the market is pricing FCF decline; "cash burn" means trailing FCF is negative and the pillar is floored.
Flags (each deducts 4 points): Sell consensus — the street is outright negative · Payout >90% — dividend consumes nearly all earnings · Leverage — debt/equity above 2.5x · Unprofitable — negative trailing earnings or margin · No earnings data — profitability could not be determined · Negative equity — book-value metrics unreliable (usually buyback-driven; quality is scored on ROA so these names are ranked, not excluded) · Weak momentum — bottom-decile 12-1 momentum, the classic value-trap marker · Thin coverage — fewer than five analysts · Sparse data — thin inputs, treat the score as low confidence · Target looks stale — implied upside above 150%, usually an unrevised target, so the upside pillar was not scored.
Analyst / Rating / Rated — the last 5 individual rating actions on record, newest first: the covering firm, the grade it assigned, and the date. These are individual firms' calls, not the consensus — the Consensus column is the aggregate view, and the two often disagree. Coverage depth varies, so some names list fewer than 5.
